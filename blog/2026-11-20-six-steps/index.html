The six steps I still use.

Listen. Research. Strategize. Execute. Evaluate. Growth. It isn’t a slogan. It’s the order of the work.

By Brandon Gil · 11 min read

Key takeaways

  • The order is Listen, Research, Strategize, Execute, Evaluate, Growth. I still use it, in that order.
  • Listen and Research come before ads, emails, or pages. If I skip them, I end up decorating a guess.
  • Strategy is a choice: one path for this season, and a clear list of what I will not do.
  • Execution has to be something a person can use — a page, an offer, a follow-up, a set of ads that match.
  • Evaluation asks a plain question: did people take the next step, and if not, where did they stop?
  • Growth is turning the volume up only after the path works, then listening again. It’s a loop, not a finish line.

There’s a six-step process I wrote down in 2018 that I still use: Listen, Research, Strategize, Execute, Evaluate, Growth.

I didn’t write it for a pitch deck. I wrote it because I kept watching good work happen in the wrong order. A brand would jump to ads before anyone had really heard the customer. A strategy would get built on numbers nobody had measured. Execution would start before anyone had picked a path — or worse, three paths at once. Evaluation would show up as a monthly meeting instead of a habit. And “growth” would mean turning the volume up on a path that wasn’t working yet.

It isn’t a slogan. It’s the order of the work.

I still use it on Gil Marketing work: ad creative and ad management, plus the email and landing pages that have to carry the same story. The steps didn’t get more complicated. The work around them did. Platforms change. Formats change. The order holds.

Brands I’ve operated did $17M+ in revenue at a 5.5x blended ROAS. I’m not going to pretend a six-word list produced that by itself. What it did was keep me from skipping the unglamorous parts — listening, measuring, choosing, and checking — just because ads are more fun to ship.

Why does the order of the work matter?

The order matters because speed in the wrong direction costs more than going slow on purpose. I wrote this down in 2018 for that reason. I was already in the work — making ads, managing spend, touching the site, watching what email did after the click. The pattern that bothered me wasn’t laziness. When a week got tight, the first things to disappear were listening and evaluation. We’d jump to execution because execution is visible. You can show a new ad. You can’t as easily show that you sat with a customer’s words and didn’t flinch.

So I wrote the six steps in a note and I kept it. When a project starts to wobble, I don’t add a seventh step. I ask which one I skipped.

It isn’t a slogan. It’s the order of the work.

1. Listen

Listen for the job that isn’t getting done, and write it back in the other person’s words. Not the job we wish they hired the product for. The one they’re actually trying to get done.

I listen in the obvious places: customer support threads, reviews, chat logs, sales calls, the questions people ask before they buy, the reasons they give when they don’t. I also listen to the founder. They usually know where the business hurts. They don’t always have the language the customer uses.

The test is whether I can write the problem back in words the other person would recognize. If my version is full of category jargon and theirs is “I just need this to work on a Tuesday,” I haven’t listened yet.

This step saves more wasted creative than any hook formula I know. Ads that use the customer’s language don’t have to shout. Emails that answer the real objection don’t have to bribe. Pages that speak to the actual job don’t have to hide behind atmosphere.

If I skip Listen, I end up making work that sounds like a marketing site talking to itself.

2. Research

Research what’s already public — the site, the emails, the product, the ads that are already running — and keep facts separate from hope. This is the unromantic part, and it is the part that keeps a plan honest.

I click through the site on a phone. I join the email list and see what actually shows up. I look at the offer, the landing path, the follow-up, the speed, the claims, the checkout. I look at what’s being said in the ad account and whether the page keeps that promise. I read the reviews again, this time for patterns, not for a single quote I can drop into an ad.

And I keep facts separate from hope. If a number isn’t measured, it doesn’t go in the plan. I would rather have a short plan with a few true numbers than a long plan decorated with figures nobody can point to in an account.

Hope shows up in familiar clothes:

  • “Our conversion rate should be higher” — should, according to whom, and compared to what we actually measured?
  • “Email doesn’t work for us” — after how many sends, with what offer, to which people?
  • “We already tried creatives” — three ads in one week is not a creative program.

Research is also where I decide whether I can help. If the product, the margins, or the operations can’t support the growth someone wants, no amount of ads will make that polite. I’d rather say so early.

This is the same instinct I wrote about in I wouldn’t start with ads: get a clear picture of the path after the click before you decide the problem is “we need more traffic.”

3. Strategize

Strategize by picking one path for this season and saying what you will not do. A strategy that tries to do everything is a to-do list. I want a choice.

One path might be: tighten the offer, match the page to the ads, install a real welcome and cart follow-up, then scale the creative that’s already telling that story. Another path might be: rebuild a landing page around one bundle and run ads only to that page until we know if the offer holds. The point isn’t that there’s a universal right path. The point is that there is one for this season, and we can name it.

Saying what you will not do is the part most teams skip. We will not launch a second storewide promo while the first offer is still fuzzy. We will not stand up four new channels because a competitor is “on TikTok.” We will not redesign the whole site to avoid writing a better headline.

Constraints make the execution honest. They also make evaluation possible. If you did five things at once, you don’t get to claim you learned from the week.

4. Execute

Execute the path: the page, the offer, the follow-up, the ads. Something a person can actually use. I care about this more than I care about the deck that described it.

Execution, for the work I do, usually looks like a short set of tangible things:

  • An offer you can say in one breath
  • A page that continues the line the ad will use
  • Ads — static and video — that carry that same promise, built and managed in the same loop
  • Email and SMS that welcome, then help, and don’t contradict the page
  • Tracking that can see the next step, even if the dashboard isn’t fancy

I run creative and growth marketing as one job, not two tickets in two queues. If the ad and the page drift, that’s an execution failure, not a “channel” failure.

I also won’t hide unfinished work behind polish. A clear landing page with a straight offer beats a beautiful page that still asks the visitor to figure out what to do. A simple welcome email that ships beats a twenty-email map that lives in a slide.

5. Evaluate

Evaluate honestly. Did people take the next step? If not, where did they stop? Evaluation is not a vibe and it is not a vanity metric. I want a straight answer to a straight question.

We asked someone to do a thing — add the bundle, subscribe, start checkout, place the order. Did they do it? If they didn’t, where did they leave?

This is where teams get soft, including smart ones. We look at the metric that moved in the direction we hoped, and we ignore the one that tells a harder story. We call a campaign a win because the hook rate was strong, even if nobody could find the offer on the page. We call email “underperforming” when the offer in the email isn’t the offer on the site.

Honest evaluation also means I don’t invent a result. If I didn’t measure it, it doesn’t go in the recap. I’d rather have a short, true read than a story.

When the next step isn’t happening, I don’t immediately ask for more budget or more ads. I go back to the path: offer, page, follow-up, friction. That’s analytics and CRO in plain language. Then I decide whether the strategy still holds, or whether we learned that this path isn’t the one.

6. Growth

Growth is turning the volume up only after that path works, then listening again. This is the step everyone wants to start on. I want it too.

Growth is more of the thing that’s working: more creative variations on a line that’s already honest, more budget behind ads that land on a page I trust, more of the email that actually helps, maybe another channel once the first one is stable.

It is not “do more of everything.” It is not a new strategy every Monday. And it is not a finish line. The last move in the sequence is to listen again — to the new customers you just acquired, to the new objections, to the new leak that only shows up when volume arrives.

That’s why I draw this as a loop, not a ladder. Growth without another pass through Listen and Evaluate is how a brand scales a story that’s already drifting.

If you only remember one thing from this post, remember the order. Do the unglamorous steps first. Then turn the volume up.

A one-page brief you can fill in today

  • Listen: write the customer’s job in their words, not yours. If they wouldn’t recognize the sentence, you’re not done.
  • Research: list only what you can point to on the site, in the inbox, or in the ad account. If a number isn’t measured, it stays out of the plan.
  • Strategize: name one path for this season, and three things you will not do.
  • Execute: ship the offer, the page, the follow-up, and the ads that all say the same thing.
  • Evaluate: did people take the next step you named? If not, mark the exact place they stopped.
  • Growth: raise volume only on the path that held, then listen again to the new customers you just acquired.

What does this look like on a real engagement?

When someone talks to me about working together, I run this sequence in the background, even on a short call. I listen for the job they can’t get done, I research what I can see in public, and I suggest one path, not five.

The job is usually some mix of “the ads aren’t holding,” “the page doesn’t convert,” “we don’t have a peak-week plan,” or “email is an afterthought.” If we work together, we execute that path in their accounts, we evaluate against the next step we named, and we grow what’s working.

If it’s a peak-season project, the same order still applies. The Black Friday launch sprint is built that way: offer and brief first, then email, sale page, and ad creative, then a go-live, then a recap. The calendar is compressed. The order isn’t skipped.

And if the right move is “don’t spend more until the path is fixed,” I’ll say that. I’d rather be useful than busy.

Questions I get asked

Is this just a marketing framework with nicer words?

It’s an order of operations. I still use it because skipping a step has a predictable cost: ads that don’t match a page, strategy with no facts, growth on a leaky path.

How long should each step take?

It depends on the mess in front of us. Listen and Research can be days, not months, if we are honest and focused. Strategy should be short enough to write on one page. Execution and evaluation should overlap once something is live. I don’t use the steps as an excuse to delay shipping.

Do I have to pause ads to do this?

Usually no. You can hold spend at a level you can afford to learn from, run the sequence, and then grow the combinations that work. Pausing everything is rarely required and often just resets the learning.

Where do ad creative and ad management fit?

In Execute, Evaluate, and Growth — with Listen and Research feeding the work. I don’t separate “the people who make the ads” from “the people who run the ads,” and I don’t separate either from the page and the email those ads send people to.

What if we already know the strategy?

Then show me the research it stands on, and the next step you’ll evaluate. If those are solid, we can move. If “we already know” is a way to skip listening, we’ll pay for that later, in the ads or on the page.

Can I use this if I don’t work with you?

Yes. That’s why I published it. Use the order. Keep facts separate from hope. Turn the volume up after the path works.
