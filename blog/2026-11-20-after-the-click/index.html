I wouldn’t start with ads.
I’d fix what happens
after the click.

Ads buy attention. They don’t fix a fuzzy offer, a mismatched page, or a missing follow-up.

By Brandon Gil · 12 min read

If I were starting a DTC brand this quarter, I wouldn’t start with ads.

I know how that sounds. I write ad creative and I manage the accounts those ads live in. Ads are a big part of the work. They’re just the wrong first move.

Because ads buy attention. They don’t fix what happens after the click.

I’ve sat in enough ad accounts to recognize the pattern. Spend goes up. The creative looks sharper than last month. And the conversion rate barely moves. The room wants more ads, a new hook, a different test, a bigger budget. Sometimes that’s the right call. A lot of the time, it isn’t. The leak is downstream. The offer is fuzzy. The landing page tells a different story than the ad that earned the visit. Nobody wrote the follow-up, so the people who didn’t buy on the first pass disappear. More spend just sends more people into the same fog.

Brands I’ve operated did $17M+ in revenue at a 5.5x blended ROAS. That number didn’t come from buying traffic first and hoping the site would figure it out. It came from getting the path after the click straight, then turning the volume up.

Why starting with ads feels right

Starting with ads feels right because it is the lever that looks fastest. If you run a Shopify brand, you can launch a campaign this afternoon. You can see spend, clicks, and add-to-carts by tonight. Creative is visible. Management is visible. A founder can point at a new ad and feel like the business moved.

I get it. I do that work on purpose. Gil Marketing is built around ad creative and ad management, and I also do the email and the landing pages, because those are the parts that decide whether the click was worth buying.

The problem is what the dashboard hides. A cheap click into a confused page is not a cheap click. It’s a quiet refund on the brand. The person who landed, didn’t recognize the promise, and left is harder to win back than the person who never saw the ad.

So I don’t treat “make more ads” as the default first move. I treat it as the last move in a short sequence.

The four things I get straight first

I get four things straight before I buy more traffic: the offer, the page, the follow-up, and the leak I can see. The sequence is simple on purpose. I want something a founder can walk in a day, not a forty-page audit.

  1. Make the offer obvious. One promise, one next step.
  2. Make the page continue the line that earned the visit.
  3. Write the follow-up — welcome, then help — before buying more traffic.
  4. Watch the next step (add to cart, subscribe, or bounce) and fix leaks before raising spend.

Only then do I buy more traffic.

1. Make the offer obvious

An offer is the reason a person should take the next step, said plainly enough that they don’t have to decode it. It is not a discount by default, and it is not a slogan.

When an offer is fuzzy, everything downstream gets harder. The ad has to over-explain. The page has to invent a story the ad didn’t tell. Email has to apologize for the confusion. I’ve watched brands try to spend their way out of that, and the spend just multiplies the fog.

What I want to be able to say, out loud, in one breath:

  • Who this is for
  • What changes for them
  • What they should do next
  • What they get when they do it

If I can’t say that without a slide, the offer isn’t ready for traffic.

A few tells I look for on a store:

  • The homepage promises one thing, the product page promises another, and the ad promises a third.
  • The “offer” is a percentage off, with no reason it exists this week and no next step after the code.
  • The hero asks the visitor to “learn more,” “explore,” or “shop the collection” — three ways of saying we haven’t decided.

I don’t need a complicated stack to fix this. I need one promise and one next step, written in the language a customer already uses. Then the ad, the page, and the first email can all repeat it.

If you’re heading into a peak week, this matters more, not less. A Black Friday campaign that discounts a fuzzy offer is still a fuzzy offer, just louder. That’s why the Black Friday launch sprint starts with the offer and the one-page brief, not with a pile of ads.

2. Make the page continue the line that earned the visit

The page should feel like the next sentence of the ad, not a different conversation. That’s message match, and it is one of the highest-leverage fixes I know that doesn’t require more budget.

Someone tapped an ad because a specific line, image, or offer caught them. If the landing page opens with a generic brand film, a different product, or a headline that could belong to any store in the category, you made them start over. A lot of them won’t.

This is where I see creative and management split in a way that hurts the brand. A team makes ads in one lane. Another team “owns the site.” The media buyer gets blamed for a CPA that was decided by the page. I don’t work that way. If I write the creative, I also want a say in where it lands, and I want the landing page — or the product page the ad points to — to carry the same promise, the same proof, and the same next step.

A practical check I run before I scale a winner:

  • Does the first headline on the page use the same promise as the ad, or a close cousin?
  • Is the product in the ad the product on the page?
  • Is the offer in the ad actually present, above the fold, with the same terms?
  • Is the next step the same next step? If the ad said “get the bundle,” the button should not say “shop all.”
  • Can a visitor understand the deal in five seconds on a phone?

If the answer is no, I don’t pour more budget into the ad. I fix the page, or I build a focused landing page that can tell the same story. That’s Shopify and ecommerce work and conversion work, not a media-buying trick.

The ads usually get better when the page is clear, too. Once you know the sentence the page can keep, you know which hooks are honest and which ones will cause a bounce.

3. Write the follow-up before you buy more traffic

Write the follow-up before you raise spend, because most people don’t buy the first time they click. That’s normal. What’s not normal is paying for the click and then having nothing useful to say on day one, day three, or day seven.

I don’t mean a full quarter of campaigns. I mean the minimum path:

  • A welcome that restates the promise and helps them take the next step
  • A browse or cart follow-up that is actually helpful, not nagging
  • A post-purchase note that makes the first order feel like the start of a relationship, not a receipt

“Welcome, then help” is the rule I keep coming back to. The first email should not be a coupon blasting over a broken promise. It should sound like the ad and the page, and it should be useful.

This is email and retention work, and it’s part of the same system as the ads. If I only manage the media and never touch the inbox, I am choosing to lose a large share of the people I already paid to meet.

A few rules I won’t violate just to launch faster:

  • The first message has to match the offer they clicked. If they came in on a bundle, don’t welcome them with a different hero product and no mention of the bundle.
  • Help before the hard sell. A short how-to, a fit guide, a “here’s how to use it,” or a straight answer to the question your support team hears every day will do more than a second discount.
  • If SMS is in the mix, it should carry the same promise, not a louder version of a different one.

If those flows aren’t in place, buying more traffic is how you pay to fill a leaky bucket and then complain the bucket is leaky.

4. Watch the next step and fix leaks before raising spend

I pick one next step and I watch it — add to cart, subscribe, start checkout, whatever the offer asked for — then I look at where people stop. This is not a thirty-metric dashboard exercise. It’s a short list.

  • Are people landing and leaving immediately? That’s usually message match, speed, or a promise that wasn’t on the page.
  • Are they scrolling and not clicking? That’s usually a weak offer or a muddy next step.
  • Are they adding to cart and dropping in checkout? That’s usually friction, surprise costs, trust, or an offer that changed mid-path.

I fix the leak I can see before I raise spend. Otherwise I’m buying a more expensive version of the same problem.

Management matters here as much as creative. A media buyer who only looks at hook-rate and CPM will scale an ad that is “working” in the platform and failing after the click. Because I run both the creative and the account, I want those two views in the same conversation: did the ad earn the click, and did the path deserve it?

When I don’t have a clean number yet, I don’t invent one. I would rather say “I don’t know yet” than decorate a plan with a figure I didn’t measure. This is a sequence, not a case study, and I’m not attaching results I haven’t measured on your store.

How this changes the ads themselves

Getting the path straight doesn’t make ads less important. It makes them more honest. Once the offer, the page, and the follow-up are aligned, creative has a job it can actually do.

The hook can be specific. The proof can be the same proof the page will show. The call to action can point at a real next step. Tests get cleaner, because you’re not testing five stories at once across a page that tells a sixth.

It also changes how I manage the account. I don’t treat a cheap click as a win if the landing path is broken. I don’t kill a promising ad because the page wasn’t ready. I hold spend at a level that lets me learn, fix the path, and then I scale the combination — the ad plus the page plus the follow-up — not the ad alone.

That’s what I mean when I say I do ad creative and ad management. The two jobs are one loop. Creative without a working path is decoration. Management without a working path is just spend.

When the path works, then I buy more traffic

When the path works, then I buy more traffic. That last step is the one founders want to jump to, and I want to get there too. That’s where the volume is.

But “more traffic” only pays when the path can hold it. When I see the next step happening in a way I trust — people understand the offer, the page keeps the promise, the follow-up is doing its job — then I turn the volume up. More creative variations on the line that’s working. More budget behind the ads that already match a page I believe in. More of the same story, not a new story every week.

Questions I get asked

Should I ever start with ads?

If the offer is clear, the page already continues the ad, and the follow-up is written, yes — then ads are how you turn a working path into volume. If those aren’t true, ads will mostly teach you that the path is broken, at a cost.

Does this mean creative doesn’t matter?

No. Creative is often the difference between being ignored and earning the click. I just won’t ask creative to compensate for a page or an offer that can’t keep the promise.

What if I already have spend running?

Don’t shut the account off to go find yourself. Hold the budget at a level you can afford to learn from, fix the four things in this post, and then scale the combinations that actually hold up after the click.

Do I need a separate landing page for every ad?

Not every ad. You need a page that keeps the promise of the ad it’s paired with. Sometimes that’s a focused landing page. Sometimes it’s a tighter product page. The test is message match, not page count.

Where does email fit if you run ads?

I’m not only an ads company. I run ad creative and ad management, and I also do email and landing pages, because the click is the start of the path. Email is how you talk to the people who weren’t ready on the first visit.
